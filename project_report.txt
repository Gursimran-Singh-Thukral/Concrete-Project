1. Introduction
Dams are critical infrastructure used for water storage, irrigation, hydropower generation and flood control. Their structural condition must be inspected regularly to identify defects before they develop into serious problems.
Traditional underwater dam inspection often requires trained divers, specialized equipment or partial lowering of reservoir water levels. These approaches have limitations involving operational risk, visibility, cost, accessibility and inspection time.
This project proposes a compact, low-cost underwater remotely operated vehicle (ROV), primarily manufactured using 3D printing, capable of navigating near submerged concrete dam surfaces and capturing images for defect identification.
The initial prototype is intended for testing at approximately 1 metre underwater, while a depth of 30 metres remains a longer-term design target.
The project also aims to integrate computer vision and artificial intelligence to identify visible cracks in concrete surfaces.
1.1 Project objectives
The proposed system aims to:
1. Design and manufacture a compact underwater vehicle using additive manufacturing.
2. Develop an effective underwater propulsion system.
3. Establish a reliable control and communication method.
4. Capture images of submerged concrete surfaces.
5. Identify visible cracks through an AI-based image-analysis system.
6. Document suspected defects for subsequent assessment.
7. Investigate additional inspection methods that could improve damage characterization.
8. Reduce reliance on direct diver involvement for preliminary visual inspections.
The system is intended as an inspection-support tool, not a replacement for professional dam-safety assessment.

4.1 Hydrostatic pressure
Pressure due to water depth is calculated using:
\[
P=\rho gh
\]

where \(\rho\) is water density, \(g\) is gravitational acceleration and \(h\) is water depth.
Using freshwater density of approximately 1000 kg/m³ and \(g=9.81\) m/s²:
Parameter	1 m	30 m
Gauge hydrostatic pressure	9.81 kPa	294.3 kPa
Pressure in MPa	0.00981	0.2943
Gauge pressure in bar	0.0981	2.943
Approximate absolute pressure	1.111 bar	3.956 bar
The external hydrostatic gauge pressure at 30 m is 30 times that at 1 m.
However, pressure-resistant housing design does not scale linearly with pressure. External-pressure buckling, fabrication defects, geometry, layer adhesion, connections and seals must also be considered.
The preliminary wall-thickness calculations in the original presentation are useful first estimates, not proof that the proposed enclosure can withstand 30 m submergence.
4.2 What changes when the vehicle moves from air into water?
Buoyancy: Water exerts an upward force equal to the weight of displaced water. The vehicle's mass distribution and displaced volume determine whether it floats, sinks or approaches neutral buoyancy.
Propulsion: Water imposes much greater loading on a propeller than air. The motor, ESC and propeller must operate as a compatible system under submerged conditions.
Communication: Conventional high-frequency radio signals become strongly attenuated underwater.
Imaging: Water absorbs and scatters light. Suspended particles, poor illumination, reflections and refraction through camera housings can reduce image quality.
Electrical protection: Batteries, connections and electronics must be protected against leaks and short circuits.
Structural loading: Water pressure acts on the enclosure from outside, potentially causing deformation and buckling.
These considerations remain relevant even for the 1 m prototype.

6.4 Camera selection for actual crack detection
Camera resolution alone is not sufficient to determine whether a crack can be detected.
Image quality depends on:
- Physical crack width.
- Distance between the camera and the concrete wall.
- Lens field of view.
- Image resolution and focus.
- Water turbidity.
- Lighting and exposure.
- Motion blur and vehicle vibration.
- Training data that resemble actual underwater footage.
For your prototype, the appropriate starting test is to image concrete specimens containing known cracks from controlled distances in freshwater and determine which cracks remain visibly distinguishable.
Only after those tests can you identify the minimum necessary camera resolution.

7. Ultrasonic sensing and crack-depth measurement
This was one of the most important technical problems identified during our discussion.
Initially, the plan was to install an ultrasonic sensor to determine the depth of cracks in dam walls.
However, measuring the depth of a crack inside concrete is different from measuring the distance between the vehicle and the wall.
7.1 Three separate measurements
Measurement	Meaning	Suitable sensing approach
Vehicle depth	How far below the water surface the ROV is operating	Water-pressure sensor
Wall distance	Distance between the vehicle and the concrete surface	Suitable underwater acoustic ranging system
Crack depth	How far a crack extends into the concrete	Specialized non-destructive testing
These measurements should not be confused.
7.2 What ultrasonic frequency is used?
Concrete ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) measurements commonly use transducers operating at frequencies in the tens of kilohertz. A frequency around 54 kHz is widely used for general concrete testing.
However, 54 kHz is not automatically the correct frequency for an underwater dam-mounted crack-depth device.
The method depends on the concrete's characteristics, ultrasonic transducer, propagation path, coupling conditions, measurement geometry and the particular defect.
The ASTM C597 standard addresses ultrasonic pulse velocity testing of concrete.
7.3 Why underwater crack-depth measurement is difficult
Conventional concrete UPV arrangements usually depend on a known transducer configuration and adequate acoustic coupling.
Additionally, cracks that are filled with water can transmit acoustic energy differently from air-filled cracks.
Consequently, a conventional crack-depth interpretation may become misleading if its assumptions are not satisfied.
For reliable quantitative measurements, the project would need an appropriate underwater-compatible non-destructive testing arrangement, calibration specimens and experimental validation.
7.4 Decision for Phase 1
Quantitative crack-depth measurement will not be a mandatory objective of the 1 m prototype.
The immediate objective will instead be:
- Detecting visible crack candidates.
- Documenting their apparent location and orientation.
- Recording comparable images.
- Identifying visible deterioration.
- Supporting repeat inspections.
Ultrasonic internal-defect characterization remains a possible future phase, not a demonstrated feature.

8. What determines dam health?
A major question raised during project development was whether dam health should be evaluated through the number of cracks, their depth or other parameters.
The conclusion was that crack count alone cannot establish the structural condition of a dam.
Actual dam-health assessment includes several interacting forms of evidence.
8.1 Surface cracking
Relevant characteristics include the presence, approximate length, width, orientation, location, distribution and progression of cracks.
A single major structural crack may be more significant than numerous small superficial cracks. The interpretation depends on the crack's location, mechanism and surrounding structural conditions.
8.2 Concrete deterioration
Surface spalling, erosion, abrasion, exposed reinforcement, corrosion staining and other forms of degradation may reveal additional problems.
A camera-based inspection system could potentially identify some visible forms of deterioration, provided that suitable images and training data are available.
8.3 Seepage and leakage
Unexpected water flow through joints or defects can indicate potential internal or hydraulic problems.
Not every visible crack causes leakage, and leakage cannot always be detected by imaging alone, particularly underwater.
Specialized investigation may be necessary.
8.4 Displacement and deformation
Unusual movement of structural elements may indicate a more serious condition.
An ordinary camera recording does not independently provide high-accuracy structural displacement measurements. That would require suitable survey techniques, reference points or specialized monitoring instruments.
8.5 Change over time
Repeated inspections can be especially valuable.
For example, the system could document whether a previously observed crack has visibly extended, whether surface deterioration has increased or whether new defects have appeared.
However, consistent image capture and spatial referencing are essential for meaningful comparisons.

9. AI-based concrete crack detection
The next major development objective is to create an AI model that can accept underwater camera images and identify regions containing possible concrete cracks.
The desired application should support both stored images and live video.
Two different AI functions were discussed:
Image classification: Determines whether a captured image or image patch contains evidence of a crack.
Object detection/localization: Attempts to identify where a crack is located within the image.
The distinction matters because the provided training dataset does not contain conventional object-detection bounding-box annotations.

A critical limitation must be considered: SDNET2018 patches originate from a much smaller number of original photographs.
Randomly splitting individual patches may place visually related patches from the same original surface in both the training and testing datasets.
This can cause overly optimistic evaluation results.
For a stronger scientific evaluation, the data should be split by original photograph or source structure whenever reliable grouping information is available.

10. YOLO-based crack localization in live video
The requirement was to use a camera, display live footage, and draw bounding boxes around detected cracks.
There are two possible implementations.
10.1 Approach A — Classification with sliding windows
This is the starting approach supported by SDNET2018.
Illustration of approximate region localization. This is not an actual trained-model output.
The live frame is divided into overlapping regions. Each region is passed through the crack classifier.
The predicted scores can be combined into a coarse probability map. Regions exceeding a selected threshold are grouped, and bounding boxes are drawn around candidate areas.
This can provide a useful visual demonstration, but it does not identify the exact crack geometry.
One physical crack may produce several candidate regions, or several defects may merge into one region.
The number of boxes must not be interpreted as a reliable crack count.
10.2 Approach B — Proper YOLO object detection
True object detection requires training annotations specifying the locations of cracks.
Such annotations generally use bounding-box coordinates rather than only crack/no-crack labels.
A stronger development approach would be to acquire a crack-localization dataset with appropriate annotations or create and verify new annotations.
A segmentation model could also be explored because cracks are thin, irregular structures that are not always represented effectively by rectangular bounding boxes.
This would allow the project to progress from image classification to spatially localized visual inspection.

12. Using AI with an underwater camera
A model trained on SDNET2018 learns patterns associated with cracked and uncracked concrete surfaces photographed in air.
The underwater application introduces a domain shift.
Differences include reduced contrast, suspended particles, nonuniform lighting, color attenuation, reflections, refraction and changes in apparent texture.
Consequently, good performance on SDNET2018 does not guarantee comparable performance on submerged dam walls.

13. Proposed prototype testing methodology
The testing program should proceed incrementally rather than placing the fully assembled vehicle directly at 1 m depth.
1. Dry electrical and control testing
   Verify receiver communication, ESC operation, motor direction, camera acquisition and power connections. Ensure that motor control failsafes work as intended.
2. Enclosure leak testing
   Test the sealed, unpowered housing before installing expensive electronics. Inspect cable penetrations, seals and joints.
3. Buoyancy and stability testing
   Determine whether the vehicle floats or sinks, whether it maintains a useful orientation and whether component placement causes unwanted pitching or rolling.
4. Shallow propulsion testing
   Operate the motor system underwater in a controlled tank or pool. Observe thrust, response, current draw and stability.
5. 1 m operational testing
   Conduct a supervised, controlled test with a retrieval line, working communication system and onboard image recording.
6. Concrete imaging experiments
   Capture images of concrete specimens with known visible cracks from various camera distances and lighting conditions.
7. AI model evaluation
   Compare model predictions against manually established defect labels. Document missed cracks, false alarms and processing speed.

16. Limitations of the proposed system
The current project has several important limitations.
Mechanical: The enclosure has not been shown to meet a complete validated pressure-resistance requirement. Waterproofing and propulsion performance also require physical tests.
Electrical: Final component specifications, compatible ESCs, electrical protection and safe battery integration remain to be confirmed.
Communication: Fully submerged conventional RC communication cannot be assumed reliable.
Imaging: A low-cost camera may not detect fine cracks, particularly in turbid water or under poor illumination.
Ultrasonic sensing: Internal crack-depth measurement has not been established for the proposed underwater arrangement.
Artificial intelligence: No verified trained weights or measured accuracy are currently available. The dataset labels support classification but do not provide precise crack boundaries.
Dam-health assessment: Visual defect recognition cannot independently determine whether a dam is structurally safe.
These limitations define the future experimental work rather than invalidating the project concept.

20. Conclusion
The proposed Automated System for Underwater Dam Inspection has evolved from a mechanically focused underwater vehicle concept into a broader inspection-support project involving underwater operation, imaging and artificial intelligence.
The most important change is the decision to conduct initial testing at 1 m depth, allowing the team to investigate waterproofing, propulsion, buoyancy, communication and camera performance before attempting deeper deployment.
The second important change concerns sensing. Basic water-depth measurements, vehicle-to-wall distance measurements and internal crack-depth measurements are different engineering problems. For initial testing, quantitative crack-depth measurement is not essential.
The third important development is the identification of appropriate visual inspection objectives. Dam condition cannot be determined solely from the number of visible cracks. Crack geometry, surface deterioration, seepage, structural movement and changes over time are all relevant to dam-health assessment.
Finally, SDNET2018 provides a useful starting point for developing an AI crack classifier. However, a reliable underwater crack-localization system still requires successful training, independent evaluation, representative underwater images and appropriate localization annotations.
The immediate project objective should therefore be to demonstrate a functional 1 m underwater vehicle capable of capturing useful concrete-surface imagery, followed by measured development of AI-assisted crack recognition.
Successful completion of these stages would provide an experimental basis for investigating deeper, more capable underwater dam-inspection systems.
